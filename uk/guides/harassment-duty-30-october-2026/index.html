Guide for UK employers

The duty to prevent harassment from 30 October 2026: a checklist for small employers

By Patricia Cruz, Sana Essencia · 27 October 2026

Short answer: from 30 October 2026, employers in England, Wales and Scotland must take all reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their staff, and become liable for harassment by third parties such as customers and clients. Acas suggests assessing the risks, having a policy, training staff, giving more than one way to report, and updating your customer and supplier terms [1, 2].

What changes on 30 October 2026

ChangeWhat it means
Sexual harassmentThe duty moves from taking “reasonable steps” to prevent it to taking “all reasonable steps” [1].
Third-party harassmentEmployers become liable for harassment of their staff by customers, clients, suppliers and other third parties. It covers harassment under the Equality Act except harassment related to pregnancy and maternity, or marriage and civil partnership [2, 3].
WhereEngland, Wales and Scotland. Not Northern Ireland [3].

What “all reasonable steps” means for a small business

Acas says it depends on your size, sector and type of work, and that it means doing what is possible with the resources you have. If you decide a step isn’t reasonable for you, write down why [2].

An 8-point checklist

Based on the steps Acas suggests [1, 2].

  • ☐ Assess the risks, including from customers, clients and visitors, and act on what you find.
  • ☐ Have a policy on harassment, or update the one you have. A standalone sexual harassment policy can help.
  • ☐ Train your staff, including how to stay safe and calm a situation down.
  • ☐ Give more than one way to report, so no one has to go through the person they’re reporting.
  • ☐ Record incidents and what you did about them.
  • ☐ Update your terms with customers, clients and suppliers, including booking terms.
  • ☐ Tell customers what happens if they harass your staff.
  • ☐ In higher-risk settings, consider extra staff, personal alarms or cameras.

Where it fits with stress risk assessment

Employers already have a legal duty to assess the risk of work-related stress and act on it, and to write the assessment down if they have five or more workers [4]. Harassment sits in the “relationships” area of HSE’s six Management Standards, so you can cover both in one assessment.

Official guidance

  • Acas has updated its guidance on sexual harassment and third-party harassment [1, 2].
  • The EHRC’s existing technical guidance applies for now; it has said new guidance is coming [3].
